Lunar New Year

By Arden Cha, Sara VanHorn, and Naya Agarwal

As the first new moon of the year rises on February 17, 2026, millions worldwide celebrate the Lunar New Year (or Spring Festival), marking 15 days of reunion, renewal, and hope. Dating back to the Chang Dynasty, Lunar New Year has been an amazing day to celebrate the upcoming spring and the New Year. Stories about this day have been passed down from generation to generation, sharing the unique traditions and fables that have created this holiday.

One more common fable is one about the ferocious monster Nian that emerged from the sea to eat all of the farmers. The farmers, along with the dragons, fought back against Nian using fireworks and decorating the village with red, hoping to scare off the monster. When this was successful, they celebrated by launching more fireworks and having a big feast.

Lunar New Year in China

To better understand how Lunar New Year is celebrated in China, one of the main countries in Asia that celebrates the holiday, we interviewed Kaiyao Ni, an upper school Mandarin teacher, about the core values and the ways people celebrate Lunar New Year from her point of view. Ms. Ni said that in China, everyone gets off from work or school, with the adults getting 10 days of break and the students getting 1.5 months to overlap with their winter break. Relatives and family members all come together for a family reunion. The celebration itself lasts 15 days traditionally, and is celebrated with lots of fireworks, firecrackers, and the color red, which symbolizes good luck and fortune. Ms. Ni shared that the main message of Lunar New Year to her is people's general and humble hope of family getting together and setting New Year's resolutions, while reflecting on the past year.

Lunar New Year in Korea

In Korea, Lunar New Year is celebrated similarly, but differently. One difference is Korean children all around the world bow traditionally to their elders (parents, grandparents, etc.) and say 새해 복 많이 받으세요 ("sae hae bok mani badeusayo"), which means Happy New Year. In return, they receive a white envelope usually filled with money.

Lunar New Year at EA

To celebrate Lunar New Year at EA, the Mandarin classes give an overview on the significance and history of the holiday. This is usually accompanied by a song and a skit. They do this for the representation of students and staff for people who it's an important part of their life. It is also a reminder that there is always a chance for new beginnings and a celebration of community and family.

Lunar New Year vs. Chinese New Year

Sometimes, Lunar New Year is also called Chinese New Year. This is because the holiday originated in China, but it is now celebrated in many other Asian cultures. Using the term "Lunar New Year" is broader and respects all of the other cultures who celebrate this holiday.

Lunar New Year is a big holiday, celebrated all over the world. So when you see red decorations at the time of year that Lunar New Year is celebrated, we hope that you have a better understanding of what this holiday means.
